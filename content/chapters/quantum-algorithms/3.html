The circuit's result is not explained by saying that it evaluated two inputs at once. The useful step is how two signed contributions combine into each final amplitude.

1. Add the two contributions to each output

Set a=(−1)f(0) and b=(−1)f(1). The input before the final H is (a,b)ᵀ/√2. Afterwards it is

\[\frac{a+b}{2}|0\rangle+\frac{a-b}{2}|1\rangle.\]

Output zero receives contributions a/2 and b/2. Output one receives a/2 and −b/2. Equal signs reinforce at zero and cancel at one. Opposite signs cancel at zero and reinforce at one. In either case the reinforced amplitude has magnitude one.

For table 01, a=1 and b=−1: the zero amplitude is 1/2−1/2=0, while the one amplitude is 1/2+1/2=1. For table 10 both final amplitudes are negated relative to that example, leaving probabilities unchanged.

\[P(0)=\frac{1+(-1)^{f(0)\oplus f(1)}}2,\qquad P(1)=\frac{1-(-1)^{f(0)\oplus f(1)}}2.\]

These probabilities encode the desired relation, not the individual function values. Replacing f by its complement negates every input amplitude after kickback, producing the same final distribution.

2. What if we measure too early?

Immediately after the oracle the input has equal component magnitudes for every table. A Z measurement at that point records a random input label. Conditioned on the record, the input is a basis state. Applying H afterwards gives equal final Z probabilities for either branch. Averaging the branches remains uniform.

This differs from leaving the amplitudes coherent and applying H directly. An ignored record does not mean no measurement took place. The physical branch selection has removed the relative-phase relation used for the deterministic output.

We have used ideal projective measurement to make this comparison precise. Noise and partial loss of coherence produce more general behavior, which Chapter 9 will model systematically. In a real implementation, finite sampled results should be evaluated as evidence about that implementation, not assumed to satisfy the ideal theorem exactly.

3. A reusable algorithm-design pattern

The pattern is: define a property, arrange a function-dependent relative phase, then choose a mixing transformation that concentrates the desired information into a measurable distribution. A common phase alone cannot carry the distinction. Nor does an arbitrary phase pattern automatically yield one deterministic bit string.

Deutsch's algorithm is small enough to inspect every contribution. The same bookkeeping extends to a sum over many input labels. In the next lesson, four contributions are sufficient to see Deutsch–Jozsa before introducing its n-bit formula.
