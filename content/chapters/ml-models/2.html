A classification score is not yet a probability. Logistic models introduce a nonlinear output map, while hidden layers create learned nonlinear representations.

Scores, probabilities and decisions

\[a=w^Tx+b,\qquad p=\sigma(a)=\frac1{1+e^{-a}},\qquad\hat y=\mathbf1[p\ge\tau].\]

The threshold τ is a decision choice; it need not be 1/2. At score zero the sigmoid returns 1/2. A large positive score gives probability near one. These are model probabilities, whose calibration must be assessed rather than assumed.

Binary cross-entropy on the sigmoid output has derivative p−y with respect to the score. Thus gradients can propagate through the score to weights and bias. A numerically stable implementation evaluates the loss from logits rather than taking logarithms of probabilities that may round to zero.

Why hidden layers need nonlinearities

\[h=\operatorname{ReLU}(W_1x+b_1),\qquad a=W_2h+b_2.\]

A hidden layer maps a feature vector to a new representation. ReLU applies max(0,t) componentwise. Sigmoid and tanh are other choices with different gradient behavior. Without nonlinear activations, any composition of affine layers collapses to one affine map and does not create a nonlinear decision boundary.

An XOR example makes the point concrete. On Boolean x₁,x₂, h₁=ReLU(x₁+x₂) and h₂=ReLU(x₁+x₂−1) give h₁−2h₂ equal to XOR. The hidden representation enables a relation that one affine threshold cannot separate on those four corners.

Backpropagation is the chain rule

Backward differentiation computes how the final loss changes with intermediate variables, then with each parameter. Forward propagation and backward differentiation have different purposes. Backpropagation is not a separate model family, and a correct gradient does not guarantee a good learning rate, useful architecture or generalization.
