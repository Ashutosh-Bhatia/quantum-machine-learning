Linear regression provides a complete learning example whose predictions, objective and derivatives can all be checked by hand.

A linear function of the parameters

\[\hat y=w^Tx+b,\qquad L=\frac1N\|Xw+b\mathbf1-y\|_2^2.\]

With d features, w has d components. A model is linear in its fitted coefficients even when its features include nonlinear functions of the original input. For example, a quadratic feature x² yields a curved prediction but is still linear in its coefficients.

For points (0,1),(1,3),(2,5), the parameters w=2,b=1 give zero residual. Starting at w=b=0, the MSE is 35/3. The derivatives are ∂L/∂w=−26/3 and ∂L/∂b=−6. With η=0.1, one simultaneous update gives w=13/15 and b=3/5. Calculate the new loss rather than assuming an arbitrary step must improve it.

Closed-form fitting and gradient fitting

Least squares can be solved using numerical linear algebra. The normal equations express the optimum condition; explicitly forming a matrix inverse is often a poor numerical implementation. The notebook uses least-squares solvers for a reference and compares them with hand-coded gradient descent.

If features are redundant or samples are few, coefficients may be nonunique or unstable. Predictions, coefficient interpretation and numerical conditioning are related but different concerns. Feature scaling changes the geometry seen by gradient descent and the relative penalty induced by regularization.

Beyond one training score

Add noise to the targets and exact interpolation no longer represents the objective of learning an underlying relationship. A baseline predicting the training-target mean is optimal among constant predictors for training MSE, but must be evaluated on held-out examples. A fitted slope is not a causal effect without additional assumptions about the data-generating process.

The activity lets you step gradient descent and compare current predictions with a known least-squares solution. Its small fixed dataset supports calculation, not a claim about real-world performance.
