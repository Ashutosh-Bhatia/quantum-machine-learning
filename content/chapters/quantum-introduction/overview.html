Our goal is quantum machine learning. Before we can decide how to use a quantum circuit in a model, we need to know what kind of computation it performs. This chapter gives a first conceptual and mathematical view, with examples we will develop more fully in the foundations that follow.

1. Start with the learning problem

Suppose we already have a classical learning pipeline: prepare data, choose a model, fit its parameters and evaluate predictions on held-out examples. Where could a quantum computation enter? It might provide a feature representation, a trainable transformation, or an estimate used in a kernel method. The learning task remains the reason for introducing that component.

We therefore need two kinds of understanding. We must know the behavior of the quantum computation, and we must know how its input, output and cost connect to the learning problem. A circuit that runs successfully is one part of an experiment; it is not by itself evidence that the resulting model learns better or runs faster.

The learning-loop figure in the Preface is our starting map. Classical data and targets enter a larger process that may include quantum state preparation, circuit execution and measurement. A classical optimizer can use the resulting loss estimates to update circuit parameters. Several circuit evaluations may be needed for one update.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to name the stages of this process, explain why measurement limits what we read out, and identify a few questions that must be answered before calling a quantum learning experiment useful.

2. From physical principles to a computational model

Quantum mechanics supplies a mathematical description of physical systems. Quantum computing uses appropriate physical behavior to implement computations. The subjects are closely connected, but our immediate learning task is to understand the computational model and the assumptions under which it operates.

Consider flight as an engineering analogy. Studying how a bird flies can reveal physical principles. Building an aircraft requires turning an understanding of those principles into a controlled system. Likewise, a quantum phenomenon becomes useful for computation only when we can prepare, manipulate and measure a system in a sufficiently controlled way. The analogy motivates the engineering question; it does not explain the mathematics of a qubit.

Our computational language has three recurring objects: states describe a preparation, operations change it, and measurements specify the possible recorded outcomes and their probabilities. We will study simple classical versions first, then make the quantum rules explicit. This lets us see both the similarities and the important changes.

You do not need to know how to fabricate a quantum processor to follow this language. At the same time, implementation constraints matter when we evaluate a real experiment. Noise, connectivity and the amount of repeated measurement can affect the cost and reliability of the computation.

3. A first look at a qubit

A classical bit has labels 0 and 1. A pure qubit is described by a normalized complex state vector in a two-dimensional space. Using basis labels that we will explain in detail later, write

\[|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle,\qquad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.\]

The coefficients are amplitudes. For a measurement in the computational, or Z, basis, the probabilities of the recorded labels are \(P(0)=|\alpha|^2\) and \(P(1)=|\beta|^2\). One execution of this measurement records one outcome, 0 or 1. We estimate its probabilities by repeating a known preparation and measurement, not by reading all coefficients from one qubit.

A state with both coefficients nonzero is a superposition relative to this basis. Merely having a 50/50 outcome distribution does not distinguish coherent superposition from a classical random preparation. Relative phase and its effect on later operations will give us more information.

The Bloch sphere

After omitting a physically irrelevant overall phase for the isolated state, we can represent a pure qubit as

\[|\psi\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle,\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi.\]

The angle \(\theta\) is polar and \(\phi\) is azimuthal. The corresponding Bloch coordinates are

\[(x,y,z)=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta).\]

Changing \(\theta\) changes the Z measurement probabilities. Changing \(\phi\) at fixed \(\theta\) changes relative phase while leaving those probabilities fixed. At either pole the azimuthal angle does not identify a different physical state. This picture represents a pure single qubit; a general multi-qubit state needs a larger description.

Be careful about the geometry. The Bloch coordinate z is not an amplitude. The probabilities obey \(P(0)=(1+z)/2\) and \(P(1)=(1-z)/2\). The vector and equations should be read together.

What does Schrödinger’s cat ask us to think about?

The cat is a thought experiment about linking a quantum process to a macroscopic record. In an idealized apparatus, a decay event is connected to a detector and a trigger whose state affects the cat. A joint quantum description can associate different event components with different apparatus and cat descriptions.

The question is how this description relates to the definite record we observe. The schematic does not establish that an ordinary unopened box contains a coherent superposition. An unknown classical result in an envelope is a different situation. Nor should the cat be treated as an elementary model of how a processor stores a qubit.

For our computations, we will state the preparation and measurement rules precisely and calculate their predictions. Questions about measurement and macroscopic records can then be explored with a clear mathematical model, rather than asking an analogy to carry the whole explanation.

4. How a gate makes relative phase observable

Closed-system quantum gates act linearly on state vectors and preserve their norm. A gate's matrix U is unitary: \(U^\dagger U=I\). Measurement has its own rules and should not be confused with applying such a gate.

To see why phase matters, compare the two pure states

\[|+\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},\qquad |-\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.\]

Both give 0 and 1 with equal probabilities in a Z measurement. Their relative signs differ. Apply the Hadamard gate H, whose action on the two basis states is \(H|0\rangle=|+\rangle\) and \(H|1\rangle=|-\rangle\). Expanding the sums gives

\[H|+\rangle=\frac{(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)+(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)}{2}=|0\rangle,\] \[H|-\rangle=\frac{(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)-(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)}{2}=|1\rangle.\]

We have combined amplitudes before squaring their magnitudes. One output amplitude cancels and the other survives. This is interference. H has not performed a measurement: it has changed the state so that the subsequent measurement distinguishes these inputs.

In Figure 1.4, the phase gate is \(P(\phi)=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\phi})\): it leaves the 0 component unchanged and multiplies the 1 component by \(e^{i\phi}\). Applying it after the first H prepares the displayed intermediate state.

For an adjustable phase, start with \((|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). After H, the amplitudes are \((1+e^{i\phi})/2\) and \((1-e^{i\phi})/2\). Thus the final probability of 1 is \((1-\cos\phi)/2\), although the earlier Z probabilities were both 1/2. This gives us a continuous version of the two examples.

This is a small ingredient of quantum computation, not a general shortcut for optimization. A useful algorithm must arrange its transformations so that accessible measurement statistics answer the specified question, with the required accuracy and resource cost.

5. Why describe the whole system?

For two independent pure-state preparations, a product description combines their individual state vectors. But not every joint pure state can be written that way. A standard example is the Bell state

\[|\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2}.\]

Suppose it were a product \((a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle)(c|0\rangle+d|1\rangle)\), with the product understood as a tensor product. Matching its four coefficients would require \(ac=bd=1/\sqrt2\) and \(ad=bc=0\). The first two conditions make all four factors nonzero; the latter conditions cannot then hold. This contradiction shows that the pure state does not factor. Such a state is entangled.

Measuring both qubits in Z gives matching outcomes, 00 or 11, each with probability 1/2. But an equal classical mixture of 00 and 11 gives those same Z statistics. Matching results in one basis are therefore not enough to establish entanglement.

If both qubits of this Bell state are measured in X instead, their outcomes still match with probability one. For the classical mixture, those X outcomes match with probability 1/2. Here an X measurement means applying H before the usual Z readout on each qubit. We will derive the full basis-change and joint-measurement rules in the later foundations.

These correlations do not provide a controllable faster-than-light message. A party cannot choose its local measurement outcome to encode the desired bit. Comparing separated records requires ordinary communication. We will distinguish local statistics, joint correlations and conditional descriptions carefully.

6. Return to the quantum learning loop

We can now name the pieces of a possible quantum learning experiment. An encoding prepares a state from data. Gates, some with adjustable parameters, transform it. Repeated preparations and measurements estimate an output. Classical processing turns that estimate into a prediction and a loss. An optimizer proposes new parameters and the process repeats.

Both sides of this connection matter. How expensive is preparing the data? How many circuit evaluations does training require? How much measurement is needed to estimate the output accurately? Does the model generalize beyond its training examples? What classical model and computational budget provide a fair comparison?

Quantum computation can offer different resources for particular tasks, but a superposition alone does not guarantee a speedup. An algorithmic query comparison, the runtime of an implementation and the usefulness of a complete learning workflow are different claims. We will state the assumptions behind each claim we study.

A short historical perspective

The subject connects proposals for quantum simulation and computation, algorithms with different resource requirements, and efforts to control errors and implement larger computations. This book uses selected examples to explain the ideas, rather than making an exhaustive historical survey a prerequisite for QML. Hardware counts, provider offerings and dated milestones need their own current, sourced discussion; they are not prerequisites for the calculations in the foundations.

7. From this first picture to a working foundation

This chapter has introduced several ideas early so you can see where we are going. You are not expected to have mastered their full notation yet. The next chapter starts with switches, dice and envelopes, where we can develop state descriptions and operation matrices with familiar examples. We then introduce complex numbers, amplitudes, measurement and multi-qubit calculations in a systematic way.

Use these questions to check the conceptual picture before continuing:

  1. Map a learning task. Choose a classification problem. Identify the data, target, prediction and loss, then describe where a quantum circuit could be proposed. What comparison would you need?
    Worked reasoning

    For a binary task, classical features x and labels y might feed an encoding and parameterized circuit; measurement estimates contribute to a prediction and a classical loss. This specifies a candidate model, not its value. We still need a defined data split, an appropriate classical baseline and resource accounting for preparation, training and readout.

  2. Separate the observations. Why can the states plus and minus agree in Z probabilities but differ after H?
    Worked reasoning

    Z probabilities square the magnitudes of the separate coefficients and omit their relative sign. H adds and subtracts the amplitudes before the final squaring. That sign then changes which amplitude cancels, as the worked calculation shows.

  3. Locate the boundary of an analogy. What does a sealed envelope explain, and what does it not establish?
    Worked reasoning

    It explains uncertainty about a classical record and an information update after reading it. It does not establish coherent superposition or reproduce all the state-transformation and measurement statistics of a quantum system.
