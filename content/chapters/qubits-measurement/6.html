A useful calculation begins with a clear preparation and ends with a clearly interpreted record. This final lesson asks what the evidence supports, and what it leaves undetermined.

1. Two preparations, the same Z histogram

Compare two procedures. In the first, prepare the pure state plus. In the second, use a classical fair random choice to prepare basis state 0 or basis state 1 on each trial. The second procedure is a statistical mixture, not a coherent amplitude sum.

Both have Z probabilities (1/2,1/2). For the randomized procedure, average the probabilities from its two branches: half the time the Z result is certainly 0; half the time it is certainly 1.

Now choose X measurement. Plus gives + with certainty. For basis state 0 or basis state 1, the overlaps with plus/minus each have squared magnitude 1/2. Averaging those predictions leaves the randomized procedure with X probabilities (1/2,1/2).

Matching results in one basis therefore do not identify the full preparation. The difference is experimentally meaningful through the changed measurement statistics, rather than through a claim that the qubit is secretly a classical coin spinning too quickly.

2. One result is not a statevector readout

If a Z measurement returns 0, the original preparation might have been basis state 0, plus, or many other states with a nonzero 0 amplitude. The conditional state after the ideal measurement and the state before it are different descriptions. Learning the outcome does not reconstruct the initial amplitudes.

Even an exact Z probability distribution would not fix the relative phase of a general pure state. Learning more about an unknown preparation requires an appropriate collection of measurements on independently prepared instances, with statistical uncertainty accounted for. We do not infer a complete unknown state from one physical copy or assume that an arbitrary unknown state can simply be copied.

The comparison above distinguishes two specified procedures by their predictions. One finite sampled histogram is not a certificate that a preparation has been identified with certainty. A simulator’s stored statevector and its displayed preparation-choice record are model information, not additional values extracted by measurement.

A checklist for reading a quantum experiment

  1. What procedure prepares the system, and what ideal state or ensemble models it?
  2. What measurement basis and outcome labels are used?
  3. Is every trial freshly prepared, or is a retained post-measurement system reused?
  4. Which displayed numbers are exact predictions, empirical frequencies or simulator data?
  5. Which assumptions concern ideal measurements, noise and intervening operations?

In QML, a circuit’s predictions are often estimated from many measurement records. Practice and explain

Predict first, calculate by hand, then compare your reasoning with the solution.

  1. Compare Z/X probabilities for pure plus and the equal randomized 0/1 preparation.
    Hint

    Average the predictions for the two randomized branches.

    Worked solution

    Z is (1/2,1/2) for both. X is (1,0) for plus and (1/2,1/2) for the randomized procedure.

  2. Does seeing one zero show the original preparation was |0>?
    Hint

    Find another state capable of producing zero.

    Worked solution

    No. Plus and many other states can produce the same outcome. The outcome constrains, but does not identify, the original preparation.

  3. Explain the difference between ten fresh measurements of plus in Z and ten Z measurements on the same qubit after its first Z measurement.
    Hint

    Ask whether preparation is repeated.

    Worked solution

    Fresh preparations yield ten independent Bernoulli trials. Without intervening changes, the reused system repeats the first recorded result in the ideal projective model.
