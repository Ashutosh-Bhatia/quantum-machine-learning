In Chapter 3 we learned how to manipulate complex columns. Now ask the physical question: what does such a column allow us to predict about a qubit? The answer requires both a preparation and a specified measurement.

1. Two basis labels, complex amplitudes

A classical bit has two labels, 0 and 1. For a single qubit, those labels also name the computational basis vectors. In the declared order (0,1), a pure state has the form

\[|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.\]

The entries are amplitudes, not probabilities. They may be negative or complex. Normalization makes the eventual measurement probabilities sum to one, but the probability rule itself is a separate part of our model. The state vector, together with that rule, predicts outcomes for a chosen measurement.

Some useful names are \(|0\rangle=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}\), \(|1\rangle=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\), \(|+\rangle=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\) and \(|-\rangle=(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\). A superposition is relative to a basis: plus has two nonzero computational-basis components, but is itself one member of the plus/minus basis.

Compare the classical probability column (1/2,1/2) with the amplitude column \((1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)\). The first records uncertainty about a classical label. The second describes a pure quantum preparation. They predict the same Z outcome probabilities, but they are not interchangeable descriptions. We will use another measurement to distinguish the preparations in Lesson 4.6.

2. A state is connected to a preparation

Think of preparing a known state as an experimental procedure that can be repeated. A state vector summarizes the ideal predictions of that procedure; it is not a list of classical values waiting to be read simultaneously. Nor does superposition mean that a qubit rapidly alternates between 0 and 1 in a way that an ordinary slow detector misses.

For example, we can specify a preparation with unscaled column \((1-i,2)^{\mathsf T}\). Its squared norm is 2+4=6. Dividing by \(\sqrt6\) gives a valid normalized column. Writing the unscaled column as a ket would not normalize it automatically.

This chapter uses ideal pure-state preparations. A statistical mixture, such as choosing a basis preparation using a classical coin, is described by a procedure and averaged predictions rather than one coherent sum of its possible kets. A later chapter develops density matrices for such states.

Takeaway. Always state the basis and the preparation model. Notation alone does not tell us whether entries represent classical probabilities, pure-state amplitudes or something else.

Practice and explain

Predict first, calculate by hand, then compare your reasoning with the solution.

  1. Normalize the column (1−i,2) with a positive real factor.
    Hint

    Find its squared norm first.

    Worked solution

    N=1/√(6); the normalized column is ((1−i)/√(6),2/√(6)).

  2. Which of (1/√(3),√(2/3)), (1,i), ((1+i)/2,(1−i)/2) are already unit vectors?
    Hint

    Sum squared magnitudes for each column.

    Worked solution

    The first and third are unit vectors; the second has squared norm 2.

  3. Explain why coefficients (3/4,1/4) in a classical probability column cannot be read as the same quantum amplitudes.
    Hint

    Compare sum of entries with sum of squared magnitudes.

    Worked solution

    The sum is 1 but squared norm is 10/16, not 1. A pure state with Z probabilities (3/4,1/4) could instead use amplitudes (√(3)/2,1/2).
