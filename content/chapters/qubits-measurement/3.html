Knowing the two Z probabilities is not enough to specify a pure qubit. The missing information involves relative phase. First separate it from an overall phase, which does not describe a new physical pure state.

1. Multiply the whole state by one phase

Let \(|\psi'\rangle=e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle\). Both amplitudes receive the same unit-modulus factor. For any normalized measurement-basis vector m,

\[|\langle m|\psi'\rangle|^2=|e^{i\gamma}\langle m|\psi\rangle|^2=|\langle m|\psi\rangle|^2.\]

The coordinate columns can differ, but all these measurement probabilities agree. We therefore treat the two columns as representations of the same physical pure state. For example, plus and the negative of plus differ by the global phase −1. In contrast, minus changes only the sign of one component of plus, so it is not a global-phase change.

“Global” refers to the entire state under discussion. A factor on only one component or one branch of a larger state may instead be relative. We will return to this distinction in controlled operations and phase kickback.

2. Change one component’s phase

\[|\psi_\phi\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.\]

Every member of this family gives Z probabilities 1/2 and 1/2. The common magnitudes do not mean all its members are the same pure state. To test the phase difference, use the orthonormal measurement basis \(\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}\), called the X basis.

This basis choice can be understood directly through Chapter 3’s inner products. Its outcomes are labelled + and −. Their amplitudes are \((\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt2\) and \((\alpha-\beta)/\sqrt2\), so

\[P_X(+)=\left|\frac{1+e^{i\phi}}{2}\right|^2=\frac{1+\cos\phi}{2},\qquad P_X(-)=\frac{1-\cos\phi}{2}.\]

At φ=0 the state is plus and X gives + with certainty. At φ=π it is minus and X gives − with certainty. At φ=π/2 both X outcomes are equally likely. These predictions demonstrate relative phase without requiring us to explain a new gate circuit first; Chapter 5 will connect basis measurements with gate operations.

Relative phase compares two nonzero components. At a basis state, one component vanishes, so assigning a phase to that zero component does not create a new observable relation. This will matter at the poles of the Bloch sphere.

Takeaway. Coordinate equality, global-phase equivalence and equal Z probabilities are three different tests. Always say which one you mean.

3. Does a later operation expose global phase?

Applying the same unitary operation to globally equivalent inputs preserves their equivalence:

\[U(e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle)=e^{i\gamma}U|\psi\rangle.\]

The output vectors still differ only by a common phase, so subsequent measurement probabilities agree. A relative phase between nonzero components is different: it can affect interference and the probabilities in another basis.

Practice and explain

Predict first, calculate by hand, then compare your reasoning with the solution.

  1. Classify plus, the negative of plus and minus by coordinate equality, global equivalence and Z probability equality.
    Hint

    Inspect both coefficients, not just their magnitudes.

    Worked solution

    All three have the same Z probabilities. Plus and the negative of plus have different coordinate vectors but equivalent pure states. Minus is neither equal nor globally equivalent to plus.

  2. Evaluate PX(+) for φ=0, π/2 and pi in the phase family.
    Hint

    Calculate (1+cos(phi))/2.

    Worked solution

    The values are 1, 1/2 and 0.

  3. Prove global-phase invariance for an arbitrary normalized measurement vector m.
    Hint

    Factor the phase out of <m|exp(i gamma)psi>.

    Worked solution

    The squared magnitude becomes |exp(i gamma)|² |<m|psi>|², equal to the original probability because the first factor is 1.
