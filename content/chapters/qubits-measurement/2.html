A measurement produces a classical record. The Born rule connects the complex amplitudes of the prepared state with the probabilities of those records. A count from a finite experiment is another quantity again.

1. The computational-basis rule

For normalized \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\), a computational-basis, or Z, measurement has outcomes 0 and 1:

\[P_Z(0)=|\langle0|\psi\rangle|^2=|\alpha|^2,\qquad P_Z(1)=|\beta|^2.\]

The inner products extract the amplitudes. Their squared magnitudes give probabilities. Normalization ensures \(P_Z(0)+P_Z(1)=1\). One shot records one of these outcomes, not both amplitudes. Squaring a complex number without conjugating it is not the Born rule.

Work a complex example

\[|\psi\rangle=\frac{1+2i}{3}|0\rangle-\frac23|1\rangle.\]

The first squared magnitude is \((1+2i)(1-2i)/9=5/9\). The second is 4/9. Their sum verifies normalization. The minus sign and the imaginary component are part of the state even though the Z probability table contains only real nonnegative numbers.

2. Repetition estimates a probability

To estimate the preparation’s Z probabilities, prepare the same known state afresh for each shot. Count the records and divide by the number of trials. If a batch contains \(N_1\) ones among N shots, its empirical frequency is \(\widehat p_1=N_1/N\).

For independent identical preparations with probability p, the expected count is Np. The standard deviation of the empirical frequency is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\). These facts describe the variation across repeated batches; they do not require every batch to match its expectation or to improve monotonically as N grows.

More samples reduce typical statistical uncertainty about the distribution of the specified experiment. They do not, by themselves, reveal a relative phase invisible to Z. They also do not automatically remove preparation or readout bias. Here the sampler uses an ideal model, so its only fluctuations are from finite sampling.

A simulator may display the entire statevector because it stores the mathematical model. That access should not be confused with the classical record returned by a physical measurement.

Practice and explain

Predict first, calculate by hand, then compare your reasoning with the solution.

  1. Find Z probabilities for ((1+2i)/3,−2/3).
    Hint

    Use complex conjugates.

    Worked solution

    P0=5/9; P1=4/9.

  2. For (√(3)/2,i/2), find probabilities and expected counts in 200 shots.
    Hint

    Expected count is shots times probability.

    Worked solution

    Probabilities 3/4 and 1/4; expected counts 150 and 50. Actual counts need not equal these.

  3. A batch has 58 zeros and 42 ones. What is the empirical P(1), and does it uniquely specify the state?
    Hint

    Keep the count denominator and unknown phase in view.

    Worked solution

    The empirical frequency is 0.42. A finite-batch estimate is not an exact probability, and even exact Z probabilities do not specify relative phase.
