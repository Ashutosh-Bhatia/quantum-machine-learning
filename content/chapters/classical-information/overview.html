Before asking what makes a quantum computer different, let us build a language for systems we already understand. A switch, a die and a sealed result envelope will take us from state labels to probability columns, operation matrices and complete calculations.

The chapter follows the lecture's systems-first approach: ask what information we need, choose a representation, work through a small example, and explain what the answer means. The theory, derivations and worked reasoning form the main reading path. Experiments and programming let us check our predictions.

Before we begin

Basic arithmetic and elementary algebra are enough to start. We explain coordinate conventions as we use them and include a matrix-multiplication refresher before composition. No prior quantum physics or Qiskit experience is required.

What questions will we be able to answer?

  • How can a four-setting switch use a four-component basis column and a two-bit code?
  • What does a probability vector say about a sealed result, and what changes when information arrives?
  • How can we build an operation matrix from its response to each definite input?
  • Why does applying A and then B give BA, and why can reversing the devices change the answer?

Follow the reasoning through four lessons

  1. Finite systems, states and basis labels

    Start with switches and a die. Choose labels, declare their order and distinguish coordinates from binary encoding.

  2. Probability distributions and repeated observations

    Open the sealed-envelope example, question a probability model, then work events, samples and conditional probabilities.

  3. Deterministic and stochastic operations

    Build the four bit functions and the lecture's asymmetric random rule. Derive their action on an uncertain input.

  4. Composing operations

    Follow intermediate states, combine devices and work out why their order matters.

Predict first, then calculate and compare

When a question appears in the text, try the next step before revealing the working. In each experiment you can choose the inputs, predict the result and open the calculation. The probability sampler also lets you compare a fixed model with fresh batches. The joint-table experiment shows what changes after learning one variable.
