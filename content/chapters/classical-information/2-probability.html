Suppose a sealed envelope contains a result: pass or fail. The result has been recorded, but we have not opened the envelope. How can we describe what we know about it? This question takes us from a definite label to a probability vector.

1. The envelope and our uncertainty

Let us label pass by 0 and fail by 1. Suppose our information is represented by probabilities 3/4 for pass and 1/4 for fail. In the coordinate order (0,1), we write

\[p=\frac34|0\rangle+\frac14|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}3/4\\1/4\end{pmatrix}.\]

What are these coefficients saying? They describe our uncertainty about the label inside the envelope. They do not say that the printed result is physically both pass and fail. If we open the envelope and find pass, our updated description is \((1,0)^{\mathsf T}\). We have learned the label. Opening the envelope need not have changed the result written on the letter.

A probability vector can also describe a random preparation that produces different labels on different trials. In either case, we must state what the random variable and its probabilities refer to. An unopened fixed letter and a procedure for preparing many letters are different experiments, even if we use the same numerical column.

The general definition

For ordered state labels \((a_1,\ldots,a_d)\), let \(p_j=\Pr(X=a_j)\). A complete distribution is a column satisfying

\[p=\begin{pmatrix}p_1\\\vdots\\p_d\end{pmatrix},\qquad p_j\ge0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{d}p_j=1.\]

The entries are nonnegative probabilities. Their sum is one because the labels describe mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes: precisely one of the labels applies on a trial. Thus \((0.2,0.3)^{\mathsf T}\) is incomplete on a two-state set, while \((1.2,-0.2)^{\mathsf T}\) is invalid despite summing to one.

A point mass records certainty about one label. Our basis columns \((1,0)^{\mathsf T}\) and \((0,1)^{\mathsf T}\) are the bit examples. Intermediate nonnegative weights describe uncertainty. In this chapter the coefficients themselves are probabilities; we do not square them. Quantum amplitudes and their measurement rule will be introduced later.

2. Ask a question about several outcomes

An event is a subset of the state set. Return to a die, now with distribution in face order 1 through 6:

\[p=\frac1{12}\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\1\\3\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}.\]

Before calculating, identify which labels answer the question “is the upper face even?” They are \(E=\{2,4,6\}\). Only one face is observed on a trial, so the routes are mutually exclusive and we add:

\[\Pr(E)=p_2+p_4+p_6=\frac{2+3+3}{12}=\frac23.\]

We have not created a new physical face called “even.” We have asked a question that groups existing outcomes. The complement \(E^c\) contains the remaining labels, so \(\Pr(E^c)=1-\Pr(E)\).

When two events overlap, simply adding their probabilities counts the intersection twice. The correct identity is

\[\Pr(A\cup B)=\Pr(A)+\Pr(B)-\Pr(A\cap B).\]

For disjoint events the intersection term vanishes. This is the condition that made the die-face calculation above valid.

3. Where did the probabilities come from?

A model can specify probabilities, and observations can help us estimate them. But assigning a probability deserves care. Suppose a team won seven of its last ten matches. Does that make its probability of winning the next match exactly 0.7? What if strong players have retired, or the opposing team has changed?

The historical frequency is a fact about those ten matches. Using it as the next-match probability introduces assumptions about how relevant those observations are. We should ask what conditions stayed the same and what changed. This is already a familiar concern in machine learning: a numerical estimate does not remove the need for a faithful model of the data.

For a simpler calculation, assume a fixed bit-preparation model with \(\Pr(X=1)=1/4\). Repeat that preparation independently \(N\) times under unchanged conditions. If \(N_1\) trials produce 1, the observed frequency is

\[\widehat p_1=\frac{N_1}{N}.\]

The hat distinguishes the finite-sample estimate from the model probability. With 100 trials the expected count is 25 ones. A batch might instead have 28, giving \(\widehat p_1=0.28\). That observation does not prove that the exact probability is 0.28.

Work out the expected count

Let \(I_t\) be 1 when trial \(t\) produces a 1, and 0 otherwise. Then \(\mathbb E[I_t]=1\cdot p_1+0\cdot(1-p_1)=p_1\). Since \(N_1=I_1+\cdots+I_N\), linearity of expectation gives

\[\mathbb E[N_1]=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb E[I_t]=Np_1,\qquad \mathbb E[\widehat p_1]=p_1.\]

Equal marginal probabilities suffice for this expectation calculation; independence matters for the usual variance and concentration results developed later. Our sampler assumes independent fresh trials of the same model. A drifting preparation or dependent trials may require another model.

4. When information arrives, normalize again

Suppose we learn that the weighted die's result is even. Which outcomes survive, and what should their probabilities add up to now? The surviving faces are 2, 4 and 6, in the ratio 2:3:3. Their original total probability is \(8/12\). Divide each surviving probability by that total:

\[\Pr(X=j\mid E)=\frac{\Pr(\{X=j\}\cap E)}{\Pr(E)}=\begin{cases}p_j/\Pr(E),&j\in E,\\0,&j\notin E,\end{cases}\qquad \Pr(E)>0.\]

Our conditional distribution in full face order is therefore

\[p_{\mid E}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1/4\\0\\3/8\\0\\3/8\end{pmatrix}.\]

Check the result: \(1/4+3/8+3/8=1\). We have restricted the possibilities and rescaled the remaining weights. If we had only deleted the odd outcomes, the entries would still sum to 2/3, not one.

Two variables: information about one changes the description of the other

We will need this reasoning when we discuss two qubits, so let us first work a completely classical example. Let \(X,Y\) each take labels 0 and 1. The table contains joint probabilities \(\Pr(X=x,Y=y)\), with rows indexed by X and columns by Y.

First ignore X. Summing down each column gives \(\Pr(Y=0)=5/12\) and \(\Pr(Y=1)=7/12\). Now suppose we learn that X=0. We retain only the first row. Its total is \(1/12+1/4=1/3\), so

\[\Pr(Y=0\mid X=0)=\frac{1/12}{1/3}=\frac14,\qquad \Pr(Y=1\mid X=0)=\frac{1/4}{1/3}=\frac34.\]

The probabilities for Y have changed because the new information selects a particular part of the joint distribution. This example is not independent: the conditional distribution differs from the marginal distribution. If X and Y were independent, learning X would leave Y's distribution unchanged. Try both cases below.

What if the selected event has probability zero? This elementary conditional formula is undefined. Dividing by zero cannot create a conditional distribution; an observed event excluded by our model calls for reconsidering that model.

The table above contains probabilities, so we divide by an event probability. Later we will group quantum amplitudes by a measured label and divide the surviving amplitude vector by the square root of the outcome probability. We will derive that step from normalization rather than memorize a change of denominator.

Keep the envelope's limitation in view.

Classical conditioning updates our information. It does not by itself describe a physical disturbance or demonstrate quantum coherence. The analogy helps us understand restricted possibilities and normalization; quantum measurement will need its own precise rules.

5. Work it through yourself

  1. The recorded result. With pass ↦ 0 and fail ↦ 1, what does \((3/4,1/4)^{\mathsf T}\) mean before opening the envelope? What is our updated column after observing fail?
    Worked solution

    The entries describe our probabilities for the two recorded labels. Observing fail gives the point mass \((0,1)^{\mathsf T}\). This is an information update, not a claim that opening the envelope changed the printed result or that the letter was a coherent quantum state.

  2. Validity and an event. Is \((0.1,0.4,0.5)^{\mathsf T}\) a valid distribution on (A,B,C)? Find the probability of not B.
    Hint

    Check nonnegativity and the total before adding the probabilities of the relevant outcomes.

    Worked solution

    The entries are nonnegative and sum to one. Not B contains A and C, giving \(0.1+0.5=0.6\), equivalently \(1-0.4\).

  3. Count or model? A fixed model has \(p_1=0.3\). In 200 independent trials we observe 68 ones. Calculate the expected count and observed frequency. Does this prove that the model is wrong?
    Worked solution

    The expectation is \(200(0.3)=60\); the frequency is \(68/200=0.34\). This finite observation alone does not establish an exact probability or prove the model false. Assessing a discrepancy requires an uncertainty analysis under the sampling assumptions. If conditions changed, we should also reconsider whether the fixed model is appropriate.

  4. Condition the die. Condition on faces \(A=\{1,2,3\}\). Find \(\Pr(X=2\mid A)\). What happens for the empty event?
    Worked solution

    \(\Pr(A)=4/12\), so \(\Pr(X=2\mid A)=(2/12)/(4/12)=1/2\). The empty event has probability zero and the elementary conditional formula is undefined.

  5. Return to the joint table. Given X=1, calculate the conditional distribution of Y. Compare it with Y's marginal distribution.
    Hint

    Select the X=1 row and divide by its row total, not by the total of the whole table.

    Worked solution

    The selected row is \((1/3,1/3)\) with total 2/3. Its normalized distribution is \((1/2,1/2)^{\mathsf T}\), different from the marginal \((5/12,7/12)^{\mathsf T}\). Learning X gives information about Y in this model.

What have we added?

A column can describe certainty, uncertainty or random preparation, provided its meaning is stated. We can calculate events, compare a model with finite samples, and update a distribution when information arrives. Next we ask how to describe an operation acting on such a column.
