Matching outcomes are interesting, but they are not by themselves evidence of entanglement. We must compare specified preparations using measurement choices that distinguish their predictions.

1. Two procedures that agree in Z

Procedure Q prepares Φ-positive coherently. Procedure C flips a fair classical coin and prepares either 00 or 11 on each trial. The second is an ensemble of product states, not the coherent sum (00+11)/√2.

In Z on both qubits, both give 00 and 11 with probability 1/2 each. Both have uniform local marginals and perfectly matching outcomes. This one histogram cannot distinguish the procedures.

Now measure X on both. H⊗H leaves Φ-positive unchanged, so the X records are ++ or −−, each with probability 1/2. For either classical branch 00 or 11, the two X outcomes are independent and uniform. Averaging the two branches therefore gives all four X records with probability 1/4.

\[P_Q(\text{equal in X})=1,\qquad P_C(\text{equal in X})=\frac12.\]

In Y on both, Φ-positive instead gives opposite outcomes with certainty. This sign difference matters: entanglement does not mean “the same answer in every basis.” Each local marginal is still uniform.

2. What the comparison does and does not establish

The activity compares two known preparation models. A finite sampled histogram is not an exact probability distribution, and rejecting this particular classical ensemble is not a device-independent Bell test. More general entanglement certification requires a stated measurement model, statistical analysis and suitable criteria.

For trusted ideal Pauli measurements on independent copies, an entanglement witness can make the claim more precise. Define EZZ and EXX as the average product of ±1 outcome labels. Every separable two-qubit state satisfies EZZ+EXX≤1. For a product component this follows from zAzB+xAxB≤√(zA²+xA²)√(zB²+xB²)≤1; averaging preserves the bound. Φ-positive gives 2. Observed finite-shot values require uncertainty estimates before drawing a conclusion.

3. Reconnect to the CHSH game

The introductory CHSH game uses two independently chosen settings per observer and a winning condition depending on those settings. Shared classical randomness cannot exceed a winning probability of 3/4 under the game's assumptions. The appropriate Bell-pair measurements reach cos²(π/8)≈0.8536. Repeating only the same-basis Z/X comparisons above is not that complete game.

Return to the CHSH experiment in Chapter 1 to connect its abstract strategy with joint states and basis choices. Later treatments will make observables and statistical bounds systematic. Here the central lesson is to specify the preparation, each local measurement, the joint record and the evidence needed for the intended claim.

For QML, joint gates offer correlations unavailable to product-state circuits. That is a modeling resource, not a guarantee of better accuracy or faster training. Its usefulness must be evaluated against the task, readout costs, noise and classical baselines.

Practice and explain

  1. Give XX and ZZ correlations for the classical 00/11 ensemble.
    Hint

    Use +1 for equal records and −1 for opposite records.

    Worked solution

    EZZ=1 and EXX=0, giving witness value 1, not a violation.

  2. For Φ-positive, calculate EYY from its opposite Y outcomes.
    Hint

    The product of opposite ±1 labels is −1.

    Worked solution

    EYY=−1, while EXX=EZZ=1. Same-answer intuition is basis dependent.

  3. Would 100 agreeing X records prove the exact ideal Bell model?
    Hint

    Separate finite data from exact predictions and model assumptions.

    Worked solution

    No. It supports a statistical comparison under assumptions, but finite records do not uniquely reconstruct a preparation or establish a loophole-free Bell test.
