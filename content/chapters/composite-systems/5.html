When we measure A, what happens to B? The answer requires a joint-state calculation, the recorded outcome, and a clear distinction between conditioning and averaging.

1. Group the amplitudes by the measured label

\[|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle\otimes(a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle)+|1\rangle\otimes(c|0\rangle+d|1\rangle).\]

For a Z measurement of A, the unnormalized B branch is (a,b)ᵀ for outcome 0 and (c,d)ᵀ for outcome 1. The probabilities are their squared norms. When pa>0, divide the branch by √pa. The full conditional joint state includes A's recorded basis state:

\[P_A(0)=|a|^2+|b|^2,\qquad |\psi_{A=0}\rangle=|0\rangle\otimes\frac{a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle}{\sqrt{|a|^2+|b|^2}}.\]

If a branch probability is zero, that outcome cannot occur in the ideal model; there is no normalized conditional state for it. For B measurement, instead group entries (a,c) and (b,d). Factor ordering still matters.

2. Work through a complex branch

Take ψ=(1,i,1,0)ᵀ/√3. Measuring A gives 0 with probability 2/3 and 1 with probability 1/3. Conditioned on A=0, B is (|0⟩+i|1⟩)/√2. Conditioned on A=1, B is |0⟩. B's conditional Z probabilities are therefore (1/2,1/2) and (1,0).

If we ignore A's record, average these predictions with their branch probabilities: PB(0)=(2/3)(1/2)+(1/3)(1)=2/3. This equals the original marginal obtained directly from the joint column. Ignoring the record means averaging probabilities across the conditional branches, not adding their normalized statevectors.

For Φ-positive, measuring A in Z yields 0 or 1 equally. Conditioned on A's record, B has the matching basis state. Without that record, B's local outcomes remain random. The measured joint state is a conditional branch, not the original Bell state.

3. A different basis gives different conditional states

To predict X measurement on A, first apply H on A, then use the same grouping calculation for the detector's Z outcome. For Φ-positive, positive X on A leaves B in plus, and negative X leaves B in minus. The apparatus that uses H then Z leaves A in the detector-frame state 0 or 1. Applying H again restores the input-frame X conditional state. B's conditional vector needs no such restoration.

No usable faster-than-light message follows. A's outcome is random, and B's unconditioned local statistics are unchanged by A's choice of measurement basis. To sort B's records into the conditional groups, the observers must compare A's classical outcome records. This does not mean the joint state never changes; it means the remote local distribution cannot carry a chosen message this way.

Practice and explain

  1. Measure B in Z on (1,i,1,0)/√3. Give probabilities and conditional A vectors.
    Hint

    Group entries with B=0 and B=1.

    Worked solution

    B=0 has probability 2/3 and A=plus. B=1 has probability 1/3 and A=i|0⟩, globally equivalent to zero.

  2. On zero⊗plus, which A outcome is impossible?
    Hint

    Inspect the first label.

    Worked solution

    A=1 has probability zero and no normalized conditional state. A=0 occurs certainly and leaves B=plus.

  3. For a Bell pair, compare B’s Z distribution before and after averaging Alice’s Z outcomes.
    Hint

    Weight the zero and one conditional states equally.

    Worked solution

    Both distributions are (1/2,1/2). Conditioning gives sharper predictions, but the ignored-record average does not change.
