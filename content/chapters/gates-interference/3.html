Hadamard is often introduced as a way to create superposition. That description is incomplete: the same gate can also recombine amplitudes into a definite basis state. Its action depends on the input.

1. Add in one output, subtract in the other

\[H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad H\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt2\\(\alpha-\beta)/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}.\]

The first output receives a sum; the second receives a difference. Both formulas concern complex amplitudes. From the columns, H|0⟩=|+⟩ and H|1⟩=|−⟩. Applying H to plus gives |0⟩ because the second output cancels. Applying H to minus gives |1⟩ because the first output cancels.

For plus, the two contributions to output 0 are 1/2 and 1/2; their sum is 1. The contributions to output 1 are 1/2 and −1/2; their sum is zero. Squaring contributions separately would incorrectly predict random outcomes and lose the cancellation.

2. Why two Hadamards undo each other

\[H^2\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}(\alpha+\beta)+(\alpha-\beta)\\(\alpha+\beta)-(\alpha-\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}.\]

Thus H²=I for every input, including complex states. H is both Hermitian and unitary. The intermediate plus state in |0⟩→H→H→|0⟩ is coherent, not a fair classical random choice. If a Z measurement is inserted between the gates and its result ignored, the two possible conditional inputs to the second H yield final Z outcomes with equal probabilities. The uninterrupted coherent circuit returns 0 with certainty.

This difference is central: a gate creates no measurement record. Treating an intermediate state as already measured changes the experiment. A statevector visible in a simulator is model information, not a physical observation made halfway through the circuit.

Geometrically, H exchanges the Bloch x and z coordinates and reverses y: (x,y,z)→(z,−y,x). Up to global phase it is a half-turn about the axis halfway between +x and +z. It is not simply a 90-degree rotation about y.

Practice and explain

Predict before calculating. Use the experiment or notebook to check your reasoning.

  1. Apply H to (√3/2,i/2).
    Hint

    Use the sum and difference formula.

    Worked solution

    The amplitudes are (√3+i)/(2√2) and (√3−i)/(2√2). Each has squared magnitude 1/2.

  2. Verify H†H=I by multiplying matrices.
    Hint

    The real symmetric matrix is its own adjoint.

    Worked solution

    The diagonal entries of the product are (1+1)/2=1; the off-diagonal entries are (1−1)/2=0.

  3. After the first H on zero, insert a Z measurement. Calculate final probabilities after another H.
    Hint

    The branches are zero and one, each with probability 1/2.

    Worked solution

    H sends them to plus and minus respectively. Each gives a uniform Z distribution, so their average is uniform.
