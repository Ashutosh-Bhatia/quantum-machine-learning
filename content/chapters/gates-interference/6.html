A circuit is a procedure, not just a list of matrices. We must declare the input, the order of operations and the final readout. These choices determine what the recorded bit means.

1. Time order and matrix order

Read a circuit wire from left to right. If H acts first, S second, H third and T last, then

\[|\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle=THSH|\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle.\]

The rightmost matrix touches the input first. To find the inverse sequence, reverse the time order and replace each gate by its adjoint. For example, the inverse of SH is H†S†.

Order can affect observable predictions. On zero, H then Z gives minus, whereas Z then H gives plus. They share Z probabilities but have opposite certain X outcomes. Comparing only immediate Z readout would miss the difference. Conversely, XZ=−ZX differs only by a global phase as an isolated single-qubit operation, so noncommutation alone does not always imply different single-qubit probabilities.

A complete worked sequence

\[|0\rangle\xrightarrow{H}\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\xrightarrow{S}\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}\xrightarrow{H}\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+i\\1-i\end{pmatrix}\xrightarrow{T}\begin{pmatrix}(1+i)/2\\1/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}.\]

Both final Z probabilities are 1/2, but the state is not plus: its components have a relative phase. Calculate and retain the intermediate columns before interpreting the final histogram.

2. Basis choice is a gate-and-readout procedure

A standard computational measurement records 0 or 1. To measure the input in the X basis, apply H first, then measure Z. The reason is algebraic: H|+⟩=|0⟩ and H|−⟩=|1⟩. For any input,

\[P(\text{record }0\text{ after }H)=|\langle0|H|\psi\rangle|^2=|\langle+|\psi\rangle|^2.\]

Thus record 0 is labelled X-positive, and record 1 X-negative. No gate “measures phase” by returning its value. It rotates the information into a basis whose probabilities a standard readout can reveal over repeated preparations.

For Y, define |+i⟩=(|0⟩+i|1⟩)/√2 and |−i⟩=(|0⟩−i|1⟩)/√2. Apply S† first and H second, then measure Z: HS† maps those states to zero and one. The ideal input probabilities are

\[P_Y(+)=\frac{|\alpha-i\beta|^2}{2}=\frac{1+y}{2},\qquad P_Y(-)=\frac{1-y}{2}.\]

Here y=2 Im(α*β), consistent with Chapter 4. H followed by S† is the wrong time order for this readout. If the post-measurement physical system is retained, the gates have changed its frame: a positive X readout implemented this way leaves basis state 0 after the Z detector. To restore the X-basis conditional state, apply H again. This separates outcome statistics from the physical state left by a particular implementation.

3. What will carry forward into QML?

A circuit model must specify state preparation, the ordered trainable or data-dependent gates, the readout basis and the number of repetitions. Different readout choices can expose different features of the same state. Simulator amplitudes remain a mathematical model; actual shots produce classical records.

Predict before calculating. Use the experiment or notebook to check your reasoning.

  1. Find X and Y probabilities for (1,i)/√2.
    Hint

    It is the Y-positive state.

    Worked solution

    X=(1/2,1/2), Y=(1,0). S† then H makes the Y-positive state basis zero.

  2. For U=THSH, write U† in matrix order and the inverse gates in time order.
    Hint

    Reverse products under the adjoint.

    Worked solution

    U†=HS†HT†. In time order apply T†, H, S†, H.

  3. What physical state remains after positive X readout implemented by H then Z?
    Hint

    Distinguish the input measurement basis from the detector basis.

    Worked solution

    The retained system is basis zero in the detector frame. Applying H afterwards restores plus, the ideal X-projector conditional state.
