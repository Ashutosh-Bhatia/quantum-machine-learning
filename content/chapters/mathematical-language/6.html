A closed-system quantum gate must preserve inner products and hence normalization. Unitary matrices give us the algebraic condition that enforces this for every input, not merely for one example.

1. The unitary condition

A square matrix U is unitary when

\[U^\dagger U=UU^\dagger=I,\qquad U^{-1}=U^\dagger.\]

Its columns form an orthonormal basis: the (j,k) entry of \(U^\dagger U\) is the inner product of columns j and k. Consequently, checking all column norms and their mutual inner products checks unitarity. For a finite square matrix either identity condition implies the other.

A complex worked example

\[U=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix},\quad U^\dagger=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix},\] \[U^\dagger U=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+(-i)i&i-i\\-i+i&(-i)i+1\end{pmatrix}=I.\]

Each diagonal becomes one after the factor 1/2, while the off-diagonal entries cancel. Applying U to basis state 0 gives \((1,i)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\), whose squared norm is 1. The full matrix calculation establishes more than this one-input check: it establishes the condition for every vector.

2. What is preserved?

\[\langle Uu|Uv\rangle=u^\dagger U^\dagger Uv=\langle u|v\rangle.\]

Setting u=v proves norm preservation. Orthogonal vectors remain orthogonal. For real rotations this includes preserving ordinary angles and lengths; complex unitaries preserve the complex inner product. The two-dimensional rotation picture is an illustration, not a picture of all complex state spaces.

Consider the Hadamard matrix \(H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\). It is real and symmetric, so \(H^\dagger=H\). Multiplication gives \(H^2=I\). Thus H is both Hermitian and unitary. Many unitaries, such as \(S=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)\), are not Hermitian: \(S^\dagger=\operatorname{diag}(1,-i)\ne S\).

3. Reversibility, composition and measurement

If U and V are unitary, then \((VU)^\dagger(VU)=U^\dagger V^\dagger VU=I\). Their composition is unitary, and its inverse applies the inverse operations in reverse order. This justifies describing an ideal circuit by a product of gates.

A projector such as diag(1,0) loses the second component and cannot be inverted on the whole space. It is not a unitary gate. Measurement and noisy evolution have their own descriptions; “all quantum operations are unitary” would be too broad. Unitarity describes the closed-system gate evolution considered here.

A further useful boundary is between unitary and merely invertible. The matrix diag(2,1) has an inverse, but it changes some lengths and is not unitary. Reversibility in linear algebra alone does not enforce quantum-state normalization.

Practice and explain

Attempt each calculation before opening its solution. Keep intermediate steps and state the convention you use.

  1. Check a phase gate. Verify that \(S=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)\) is unitary.
    Hint

    Conjugate the phase on the diagonal, then multiply.

    Worked solution

    Its adjoint is diag(1,−i); their product is diag(1,(−i)i)=I.

  2. Find the inverse order. A circuit applies H first, then S. Write its inverse.
    Hint

    Reverse both the gates and their order.

    Worked solution

    The circuit matrix is SH. Its inverse is \(H^\dagger S^\dagger=HS^\dagger\), so apply \(S^\dagger\) first, then H.

  3. One input is not enough. Does diag(1,2) preserving the norm of basis state 0 prove unitarity?
    Hint

    Test the other basis input or calculate the adjoint product.

    Worked solution

    No. It doubles the norm of basis state 1. Its adjoint product is diag(1,4), so it fails the all-input condition.
