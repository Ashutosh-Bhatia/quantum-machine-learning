Vectors let us keep several quantities together. The dot product tells us how two real vectors align, while the norm tells us their length. We will calculate these quantities before extending them to complex entries.

1. Multiply matching entries and add

For two real column vectors with the same dimension, define

\[u\cdot v=u^{\mathsf T}v=\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_jv_j.\]

The transpose turns u into a row; multiplying a 1×d row by a d×1 column gives one scalar. With \(u=(3,4)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(v=(2,-1)^{\mathsf T}\), the dot product is \(3(2)+4(-1)=2\). It is neither a new vector nor the entrywise product (6,−4). Real dot products are symmetric and linear in each argument.

In a real Euclidean space the same quantity has a geometric meaning:

\[u\cdot v=\|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta.\]

For nonzero vectors, an acute angle gives a positive dot product, a right angle gives zero and an obtuse angle gives a negative value. If either vector is zero the product is zero, but an angle involving that zero vector is not defined. The coordinate calculation remains valid in higher dimensions even when a drawing becomes difficult.

2. Length and normalization

\[\|v\|=\sqrt{v\cdot v}=\sqrt{\sum_j v_j^2}.\]

Our vector (3,4) has length 5 by the Pythagorean theorem. To retain its direction and make its length one, divide every entry by the same positive length:

\[\widehat v=\frac{v}{\|v\|}=\begin{pmatrix}3/5\\4/5\end{pmatrix},\qquad \|\widehat v\|^2=\frac9{25}+\frac{16}{25}=1.\]

This is normalization. A zero vector cannot be normalized, because division by its norm would divide by zero. Multiplying by a negative real number reverses direction; dividing by the positive norm does not. Unit length alone does not make a vector a quantum state of a specified system—we must also state its space and interpretation.

Classical probability normalization in Chapter 2 required the entries themselves to sum to one. Euclidean normalization instead requires the squared entries to sum to one for real vectors. Do not interchange those rules: (3/7,4/7) is a probability column but not a unit Euclidean vector.

3. Scalar and vector projections

If \(\widehat u=u/\|u\|\) is a unit direction, the signed scalar component of v along it is \(\widehat u\cdot v\). The vector projection is that scalar times the direction:

\[\operatorname{proj}_u(v)=\frac{u\cdot v}{u\cdot u}u\quad (u\ne0).\]

For u=(1,0) and v=(3,4), the scalar component is 3 and the projected vector is (3,0). The residual (0,4) is perpendicular to u. With u=(2,0), the dot product becomes 6, but the projected vector remains (3,0). Thus a dot product with a non-unit vector is not directly a projection length.

Takeaway. A dot product is a scalar, a norm is a nonnegative length, and a vector projection is a vector. Keeping their types distinct prevents many later errors.

Practice and explain

Attempt each calculation before opening its solution. Keep intermediate steps and state the convention you use.

  1. Calculate alignment. Find \((1,2)\cdot(2,-1)\) and interpret it.
    Hint

    Multiply matching entries and add.

    Worked solution

    The result is \(2-2=0\). The two nonzero real vectors are perpendicular.

  2. Normalize. Normalize \((1,2,2)^{\mathsf T}\).
    Hint

    Find the square root of the sum of the squared entries.

    Worked solution

    The norm is 3, so divide by 3 to obtain \((1/3,2/3,2/3)^{\mathsf T}\).

  3. Use a non-unit direction. Project \(v=(1,2)^{\mathsf T}\) onto \(u=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\).
    Hint

    Use (u·v)/(u·u), then multiply by u.

    Worked solution

    Here \(u\cdot v=3\) and \(u\cdot u=2\). The projection is \((3/2,3/2)^{\mathsf T}\); the residual \((-1/2,1/2)^{\mathsf T}\) has zero dot product with u.
