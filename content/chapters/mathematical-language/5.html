Chapter 2 used matrices to describe classical operations. We now make the linear-algebra rules explicit, allowing complex entries and keeping track of dimensions at every step.

1. A matrix is specified by what it does to basis vectors

An m×n matrix A maps an n-entry column to an m-entry column. If its columns are \(c_1,\ldots,c_n\), then

\[Av=\sum_{k=1}^n v_kc_k,\qquad A e_k=c_k.\]

This is the same column interpretation used for classical stochastic operations. Matrix multiplication itself is not specifically classical or quantum; the restrictions on the allowed matrices and state vectors depend on the model.

For example,

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad v=\begin{pmatrix}3\\-1\end{pmatrix},\quad Av=3\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

A is a shear in a real-plane picture. Its first basis column stays fixed and its second tilts. The same matrix acts on complex vectors too; the real drawing shows its action on real inputs.

2. Linearity and composition

A linear map obeys \(A(cu+dv)=cAu+dAv\). This permits us to transform the basis components separately and add the results. A map that adds a fixed nonzero vector is not linear: it does not send the zero vector to zero.

If A acts first and B second, the result is \(B(Av)=(BA)v\). The rightmost matrix acts first. Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\), \(B=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) and v=(1,0). Applying A then B gives (0,2); applying B then A gives (0,1). Thus the order can change the answer.

Check dimensions before multiplying. An m×n A followed by a k×m B yields a k×n BA. A matrix with two columns cannot act directly on a three-entry vector. A result with incompatible dimensions indicates a malformed calculation rather than an exotic quantum effect.

3. Transpose, adjoint and Hermitian matrices

The transpose swaps rows with columns. The adjoint, or conjugate transpose, also conjugates every entry:

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\2-i&3\end{pmatrix},\quad A^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}1&2+i\\-i&3\end{pmatrix}.\]

For an m×n matrix, its adjoint has shape n×m. A key identity is \((BA)^\dagger=A^\dagger B^\dagger\): taking an adjoint reverses the order. One way to remember it is to follow the dimensions in the reversed maps.

A square matrix satisfying \(A^\dagger=A\) is Hermitian. Its diagonal entries are real and off-diagonal entries occur in conjugate pairs. The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-i&2\end{pmatrix}\) is Hermitian. This is a different condition from unitarity. Projectors are Hermitian, yet usually are not invertible. Later, Hermitian operators will describe observables; we do not need a full eigenvalue theory to perform the calculations here.

Practice and explain

Attempt each calculation before opening its solution. Keep intermediate steps and state the convention you use.

  1. Read the columns. If \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), find \(Ae_0,Ae_1\) in order (0,1).
    Hint

    A basis input selects the corresponding matrix column.

    Worked solution

    They are the first and second columns: \((2,1)^{\mathsf T}\) and \((-1,0)^{\mathsf T}\).

  2. Check the dimensions. A has shape 3×2 and B has shape 4×3. Which composition is defined?
    Hint

    Compare the inner dimensions of each proposed product.

    Worked solution

    BA is 4×2. AB is not defined because its inner dimensions would be 2 and 4.

  3. Conjugate and transpose. Find the adjoint of \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2i\\-i&0\end{pmatrix}\).
    Hint

    Both conjugation and exchange of row/column positions are needed.

    Worked solution

    It is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-2i&0\end{pmatrix}\). Both position and conjugation matter.
