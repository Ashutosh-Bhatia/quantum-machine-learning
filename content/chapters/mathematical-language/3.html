The real dot product uses a transpose. Complex entries require a conjugate transpose. This small change makes squared length nonnegative and gives bra–ket notation its computational meaning.

1. From a ket to a bra

A ket \(|v\rangle\) is a column vector. Its associated bra is the conjugate transpose, denoted \(\langle v|=(|v\rangle)^\dagger\). First conjugate every entry, then turn the column into a row:

\[|v\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1+i\\2-i\end{pmatrix}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\langle v|=\begin{pmatrix}1-i&2+i\end{pmatrix}.\]

Conjugation and transposition commute, so reversing those two steps gives the same result. For real entries conjugation changes nothing. This is why the basis bras in Chapter 2 looked like ordinary transposes; their entries were all real.

2. The complex inner product

\[\langle u|v\rangle=u^\dagger v=\sum_j u_j^*v_j,\qquad \|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v|v\rangle}.\]

We use the physics convention: the first argument is conjugated. The product is linear in the second argument and conjugate-linear in the first. In particular, \(\langle cu|v\rangle=c^*\langle u|v\rangle\), while \(\langle u|cv\rangle=c\langle u|v\rangle\). Swapping arguments gives \(\langle v|u\rangle=\langle u|v\rangle^*\).

Why is conjugation necessary? Consider v=(1,i). Without conjugation the self-product would be \(1+i^2=0\), although v is nonzero. With conjugation it is \(1+(-i)i=2\). More generally, \(\langle v|v\rangle=\sum_j|v_j|^2\), which is positive for every nonzero v.

Worked calculation

\[|u\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix},\quad |v\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad \langle u|v\rangle=\frac{i-i}{2}=0.\]

Both vectors have unit norm. Their inner product is zero, so they are orthogonal. If we had transposed u without conjugating it, the incorrect answer would be i. The result is an error in the operation, not a different physical prediction.

3. Orthogonality, coefficients and overlap

An orthonormal basis has unit vectors satisfying \(\langle e_j|e_k\rangle=\delta_{jk}\): one for matching indices, zero otherwise. If \(|v\rangle=\sum_k v_k|e_k\rangle\), multiplying by \(\langle e_j|\) picks out the coefficient \(v_j\). That is why \(\langle0|\psi\rangle\) returns the amplitude on basis state 0.

For normalized vectors, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ensures \(|\langle u|v\rangle|\le1\). In a rank-one quantum projection onto normalized u, the probability for that outcome is \(|\langle u|v\rangle|^2\). This interpretation assumes a normalized input v and the specified measurement; an arbitrary raw dot product is not automatically a probability.

For example, \(\langle0|+\rangle=1/\sqrt2\), so the probability of outcome 0 in a computational-basis measurement of plus is 1/2. Orthogonal pure states can be perfectly distinguished by an appropriate ideal measurement; states with nonzero overlap cannot always be distinguished perfectly from one copy.

A real-plane perpendicular-arrow picture motivates orthogonality, but a general complex inner product need not be real. We use the algebraic condition of zero inner product, rather than forcing complex vectors into an ordinary two-dimensional drawing.

Practice and explain

Attempt each calculation before opening its solution. Keep intermediate steps and state the convention you use.

  1. Construct a bra. Find the bra of \((2i,1-i)^{\mathsf T}\).
    Hint

    Conjugate both entries, then write a row.

    Worked solution

    It is the row \((-2i,1+i)\).

  2. Normalize complex entries. Find positive N such that \(N(1-i,2)^{\mathsf T}\) has unit norm.
    Hint

    Add squared magnitudes before finding the normalization factor.

    Worked solution

    The unscaled squared norm is \(|1-i|^2+|2|^2=2+4=6\), so \(N=1/\sqrt6\).

  3. Orthogonality and phase. If \(\langle u|v\rangle=0\), does replacing v by \(e^{i\phi}v\) preserve orthogonality?
    Hint

    The inner product is linear in its second argument.

    Worked solution

    Yes: linearity in the second argument gives \(\langle u|e^{i\phi}v\rangle=e^{i\phi}0=0\).
