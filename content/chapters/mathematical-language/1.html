In Chapter 2, entries in a probability column were nonnegative real numbers. Quantum amplitudes need a richer number system. Before assigning those amplitudes any physical meaning, let us become comfortable calculating with complex numbers.

1. A number with two real coordinates

A complex number has the form \(z=a+bi\), where a and b are real and \(i^2=-1\). Its real part is a, and its imaginary part is b, not bi. Addition follows the coordinates: \((a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i\). Multiplication uses ordinary expansion, followed by replacing \(i^2\) with −1:

\[(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.\]

For example, \((1+2i)(2-i)=2-i+4i-2i^2=4+3i\). The minus sign in \(i^2=-1\) is essential. A complex number can be drawn as a point (a,b), or an arrow from the origin, on the complex plane. The horizontal axis records the real part and the vertical axis the imaginary part. This plane describes one complex number; it is not a Bloch sphere or the physical position of a qubit.

2. Conjugation, magnitude and an important distinction

The conjugate \(z^*=\overline z=a-bi\) reflects the point across the real axis. Multiplying by the conjugate gives a nonnegative real number:

\[z^*z=(a-bi)(a+bi)=a^2+b^2,\qquad |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.\]

The cross terms cancel and the remaining term \(-b^2i^2\) becomes \(+b^2\). This explains why we use the squared magnitude rather than the ordinary square when constructing probabilities. In particular,

\[z=\frac{1+2i}{3}:\quad z^*=\frac{1-2i}{3},\quad |z|^2=\frac59,\quad z^2=\frac{-3+4i}{9}.\]

These last two expressions answer different questions. The squared magnitude is 5/9; the ordinary square still has an imaginary component. A single amplitude with magnitude below one is not, by itself, a complete normalized quantum state: we must account for all the state's amplitudes.

3. Magnitude and phase

A nonzero point can also be described by distance r from the origin and angle φ from the positive real axis:

\[z=r(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)=re^{i\phi},\quad r=|z|,\quad a=r\cos\phi,\quad b=r\sin\phi.\]

The identity \(e^{i\phi}=\cos\phi+i\sin\phi\) is Euler’s formula. We use radians in equations: π radians is 180°. Phase is defined modulo \(2\pi\), so φ and \(\phi+2\pi\) describe the same number. At z=0, the magnitude is zero and there is no unique phase.

Multiplying by \(e^{i\gamma}\) rotates a point through γ without changing its magnitude. For example, \(i=e^{i\pi/2}\), so multiplying 1+2i by i gives −2+i, a quarter-turn. Conjugation is a reflection, whereas multiplication by i is a rotation. They are different operations.

Quantum calculations use such phase factors when amplitudes are combined. Two amplitudes can have the same magnitude but interfere differently because their phases differ. We will develop that application after learning how to combine vector entries.

Practice and explain

Attempt each calculation before opening its solution. Keep intermediate steps and state the convention you use.

  1. Calculate before simplifying. Find \((2-i)(1+3i)\).
    Hint

    Expand the product before replacing i² by −1.

    Worked solution

    Expansion gives \(2+6i-i-3i^2=5+5i\).

  2. Square or squared magnitude? For \(z=1+3i\), find \(z^*, |z|^2, z^2\).
    Hint

    Calculate the ordinary square and the conjugate product separately.

    Worked solution

    They are \(1-3i\), 10 and \(-8+6i\), respectively. Only the squared magnitude is necessarily nonnegative real.

  3. Rotate a point. Multiply \(-1+i\) by i. Does its magnitude change?
    Hint

    Distribute i across both terms, then compare squared magnitudes.

    Worked solution

    The result is \(-1-i\). Both magnitudes are \(\sqrt2\); the phase increases by π/2 modulo 2π.
